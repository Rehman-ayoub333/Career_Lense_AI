# FINAL VERDICT

**1. Is CareerLens currently strong enough for an FYP?**
As a *software engineering* FYP — yes, comfortably, once the redesign is finished and the app actually runs end-to-end. The infrastructure (API layer, error taxonomy, prompt-injection defence, rate limiting, structured logging, a real design system) is genuinely stronger than most BS-level capstones. As a *research* FYP or thesis in the sense implied by this prompt — no, not yet, and not by a wide margin. There is currently zero research scaffolding anywhere in the project: no dataset, no baseline, no metric, no evaluation script, no hypothesis, no mention of "research," "evaluation," "fairness," "bias," "F1," or "ground truth" anywhere in the 4,019-line `SPEC.md` (verified by direct grep — the only near-hit was the word "precision" inside a prompt string). `SPEC.md` explicitly optimizes for a different goal set: launch metrics, LinkedIn impressions, GitHub stars, and a scholarship SOP narrative. That is a legitimate goal for a portfolio project. It is not a thesis.

**2. Is it currently research-worthy?**
No. In its current form the AI layer is one well-engineered LLM call per feature — structured decoding, retries, one repair attempt, nonce-delimited injection defence — with no retrieval, no tool use, no verification step, no fairness testing, and no way to check whether a claim the model makes ("this skill is missing") is actually true of the source text. That is excellent product engineering. It is not, on its own, a research contribution: nothing about it could not be reproduced by a well-written prompt against any frontier model in an afternoon. A thesis needs a claim that can be falsified by an experiment. Right now there is no claim to falsify.

**3. What is the biggest missing piece?**
A way to know whether the system is *telling the truth*. Every one of "score," "skills_matched," "skills_missing," "key_actions" and "verdict" is generated by a single LLM call with no mechanism forcing it to point at the text that justifies the claim, and no gold-labeled data against which to measure how often it's right. This is simultaneously the biggest engineering gap, the biggest research gap, and — not coincidentally — exactly the property the product's own design philosophy already argues for ("a figure that cannot be verified should not be rendered at all," `ScorePanel.tsx`). The codebase has already articulated the thesis for you without noticing.

**4. What should become the central research contribution?**
**Evidence-grounded, span-verifiable CV–opportunity matching, with measured faithfulness against an ungrounded baseline — and, reusing the same harness, a measurement of whether forcing evidence-grounding reduces identity-driven score variance (a name/institution perturbation study).** Full justification in Parts 15–17. This is chosen over the alternatives (pure bias audit, pure agentic rebuild, pure skill-ontology matching) because it is the smallest change that turns an existing, real product weakness into a falsifiable, measurable, publishable-adjacent claim, and because it can be built inside the current architecture without violating the project's own stated design philosophy.

**5. What must we do next?**
Not redesign the frontend further, and not add agents. Build a 60–150-item hand-labeled evaluation set (Part 14), implement one grounding mechanism and one faithfulness metric (Parts 15, 17, 22), and run the smallest experiment that can support or refute the central hypothesis before writing a single page of thesis prose. Everything else in this document is in service of that one decision.

## 1. Executive verdict

CareerLens AI, as it exists on disk today, is two different projects wearing one repository. Project A is a genuinely well-built Next.js product: a rate-limited, abort-safe, injection-resistant, structured-decoding LLM application with a distinctive and carefully reasoned design system. Project B is what this prompt is asking for: a BS thesis with a defensible research contribution, literature grounding, baselines, metrics, and an experiment that could survive a viva. Project A is roughly 80% done. Project B does not exist yet — not "underdeveloped," *absent*: no dataset file, no eval script, no baseline implementation, no citation anywhere in the repo. This is not a criticism of the engineering; `SPEC.md` never asked for Project B, and the engineering did what it was asked. The honest statement is that the two goals were never the same goal, and treating the finished product as most of the way to a thesis would be a mistake that costs months.

## 2. Complete verified project model

**A. What problem does CareerLens AI actually solve?** It gives a CV-holder a same-session, evidence-adjacent estimate of fit against a job description or (uniquely among the named competitors) a scholarship's criteria, plus a rewrite, a cover letter, interview questions, and a chat interface — all from one pasted CV and one pasted opportunity description, with no account and no server-side storage.

**B. Who is the target user?** Per `SPEC.md` §2 (verified): final-year CS/engineering students in Pakistan, India, Nigeria, Bangladesh, Egypt applying to European scholarships or remote roles — not developers, laptop-first. The stated secondary persona is a scholarship committee member reading the founder's SOP and testing the tool. The tertiary is a LinkedIn audience. None of these three personas is "a thesis committee," which matters: the product was built to impress a *scholarship* reviewer, not a *research* examiner, and its documentation says so in writing (`SPEC.md` §1: "Why This Project Exists (The Story — Use This in Your SOP)").

**C. What does the current system actually do?** Confirmed by reading `hooks/useAnalysis.ts`, `lib/ai/index.ts`, `lib/ai/google.ts`, and `lib/prompts.ts` directly: a client submits CV text and opportunity text; the server issues one `POST /api/analyze` call (load-bearing) to Gemini 2.5 Flash with `thinkingBudget: 0` (reasoning explicitly disabled) and a JSON schema handed to the provider's constrained decoder; in parallel (`Promise.allSettled`) it also fires `/api/rewrite` and `/api/cover-letter`, each an independent single-shot call with no shared context beyond the raw CV/JD text; the results are assembled client-side into one `AnalysisSession` and written to `localStorage`. `/api/chat` is a second, wholly separate single-shot call per message — the server receives no prior conversation turns, only the current question plus the original CV/JD/score/verdict/missingSkills; conversational memory exists only in the browser's React state and is not sent back to the model beyond the current turn.

**D. Complete end-to-end data flow, as built (not as documented):**
```
Raw CV text + Raw opportunity text
  → sanitizeText() [control chars, zero-width/bidi chars, HTML tags stripped]
  → wrapUntrusted() [per-request random nonce delimiters — real prompt-injection defence]
  → ONE Gemini 2.5 Flash call, temperature 0, thinkingBudget 0, schema-constrained JSON
  → parseModelJson() + isAnalysisResult() runtime validation
  → (on failure) ONE repair call with a terse corrective instruction, then give up
  → normalizeAnalysisResult() [clamp 0–100, drop empty strings]
  → rendered directly to the UI — no independent verification step exists anywhere
  → localStorage (client only; nothing is ever written server-side)
```
There is no preprocessing beyond string sanitisation, no embedding step, no retrieval, no ranking model, no ontology lookup, and no post-hoc check that a "matched skill" the model claims is actually present in the CV text. The entire "analysis" is the judgment of one LLM call, trusted verbatim.

**E. Genuinely novel vs. commodity, sorted honestly:**
- *Genuinely novel (as engineering, not as research):* the nonce-delimited untrusted-content wrapper in `lib/prompts.ts` is a real, non-obvious fix for a documented injection vector (a CV containing the literal string `CV_END` used to defeat the old static-marker scheme) — this is good applied security thinking, though it is an engineering contribution, not a research one; nothing here is unpublished.
- *Technically interesting:* schema-first constrained decoding instead of prompt-template JSON (measurably shorter prompts, structurally impossible malformed output) — solid, current best practice, not new to the literature.
- *Good engineering:* `createApiRoute`, the error taxonomy, the two-layer log redaction, the fixed-window rate limiter with an honestly documented per-instance limitation.
- *Common implementation:* the four-endpoint LLM-wrapper shape (analyze / rewrite / cover-letter / chat) matches dozens of "AI resume tool" repositories on GitHub and several commercial products found in this audit's literature search.
- *Commodity AI functionality:* skill matching, ATS checks, interview questions, cover letters — all are things a single well-crafted ChatGPT prompt produces today, a fact several of the papers found below (Part 4) explicitly measure.
- *Merely UI polish:* the assay-hallmark score presentation, the editorial landing page — genuinely good product design, zero research content.

## 3. Attacking the project (hostile examiner mode)

**Is the problem important enough?** Yes, in the aggregate — ATS-driven resume rejection and the absence of scholarship-specific guidance are real, and `SPEC.md`'s "75% of resumes are filtered before a human reads them" figure is a widely repeated industry claim (commonly sourced to ATS vendors themselves, which an examiner will immediately flag as a self-interested statistic requiring an independent citation — currently, none exists in the repo).

**Is it clearly defined?** As a product problem, yes. As a *research* problem, no — "help someone understand their CV-job match" is not falsifiable. "Does evidence-grounded extraction reduce hallucinated skill claims relative to an ungrounded baseline" is falsifiable. The repository currently states only the former.

**Is there evidence the problem exists, beyond anecdote?** Weak. The founder's personal experience (stated directly in `SPEC.md` §1) is a legitimate motivating anecdote, not evidence for a thesis. This needs either a cited external source or the student's own small survey/interview data — currently absent.

**Is CareerLens solving the problem, or wrapping an LLM?** Presently: wrapping an LLM, competently. Every feature — score, gaps, ATS check, rewrite, cover letter, interview questions, chat — reduces to "ask Gemini a well-structured question about two pasted documents and trust the JSON it returns." An examiner who asks "what does your system do that I couldn't do by pasting the same two documents into Gemini myself with a good prompt" currently has no strong answer available in the code. That question is answerable — see Part 5 — but only after the central contribution (Part 15) is built.

**Is the problem specific enough for a BS FYP?** Yes, if narrowed to the grounding/faithfulness angle. "Build a better career AI" is not FYP-scoped. "Measure whether forcing span-level evidence citation reduces the rate of unsupported skill claims, on a hand-labeled 100-item CV/JD set, compared to the existing ungrounded pipeline" is exactly FYP-scoped: buildable in the current codebase, measurable with metrics an undergraduate can compute correctly, and small enough to finish.

## 4. The research gap (literature-grounded)

This section reports what current, real literature says, with sources. No paper below is invented.

**Resume–job matching and LLM-based matching.** Recent NAACL 2025 work directly studies LLM-based resume-job matching and its failure modes: "Human and LLM-Based Resume Matching: An Observational Study" ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.270/)) compares human and LLM judgments on the matching task itself — precisely CareerLens's core function — and a companion NAACL industry paper, "Evaluating Bias in LLMs for Job-Resume Matching" ([ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-industry.55.pdf)), built a 32,000-resume-variant benchmark across 12 LLM versions and found that while newer GPT-family models are largely fair on gender and race, **implicit bias by educational institution is the dominant, least-fixed failure mode** — Ivy League preference and bias against women's-college graduates persisted even in GPT-family models, and LLaMA-3.1-70B still showed significant institutional bias. This is directly relevant to CareerLens's scholarship mode, which explicitly scores `academic_score` and references named institutions. Other current work (GNN-based resume-job compatibility scoring, arXiv work on hiring-decision LLM pitfalls at [arxiv.org/html/2507.02087v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02087v2)) confirms this is an active 2024–2025 research area, not a solved one.

**Explainability in AI hiring/screening.** Multiple 2025 papers explicitly target "explainable, bias-aware resume screening" as an open problem, e.g. an explainable and bias-aware LLM resume-screening framework ([IJCOPE](https://ijcope.org/article/explainable-and-bias-aware-llm-based-resume-screening-system-for-fair-automated-recruitment/)) and a context-aware, explainable multi-agent resume-screening framework ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/395609711_AI_Hiring_with_LLMs_A_Context-Aware_and_Explainable_Multi-Agent_Framework_for_Resume_Screening)). The pattern across this literature: explainability in this domain is treated as *evidence traceability* (can you show which document span justifies which claim), not as post-hoc rationalization — which is exactly the gap CareerLens has, since its model is told to "cite something concrete" in the system prompt but nothing in the schema or pipeline enforces or checks this.

**Fairness/bias in algorithmic hiring, broadly.** A 2024 ACM TIST multidisciplinary survey, "Fairness and Bias in Algorithmic Hiring" ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3696457)), is the standard current reference point for this subfield (its full content could not be fetched in this session — a 403 was returned — so it is cited by title/venue only, not by claimed findings). Concrete, independently verified empirical evidence comes from Brookings' name-swapping study of embedding-based resume retrieval ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/gender-race-and-intersectional-bias-in-ai-resume-screening-via-language-model-retrieval/)): across 554 resumes × 80 names × 571 job descriptions, white-associated names were preferred over Black-associated names in 85.1% of cases where a disparity existed at all (vs. 8.6% the other way), and intersectional analysis showed Black men experiencing the sharpest disadvantage — a finding invisible if gender and race are analysed separately. This is a directly reusable methodology (perturb only the identity signal, hold the rest of the document constant, measure the outcome shift) that CareerLens's own architecture could adopt cheaply, since the pipeline already accepts arbitrary CV text.

**Hallucination and faithfulness evaluation for LLMs.** This is a mature, fast-moving area: a 2024 ACM TOIS survey taxonomizes hallucination causes and mitigation ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3703155)), and a 2025 IEEE review specifically catalogues faithfulness *metrics* for hallucination assessment ([IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/iel8/4200690/11320985/11032180.pdf) / [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00269)). None of this literature has been applied to resume/skill-gap extraction specifically in what this search surfaced — the closest work is general RAG faithfulness benchmarking ([arXiv 2505.04847](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04847v2)). That is the gap: faithfulness measurement is a solved *methodology* with no established application to career-document analysis yet.

**Agentic AI.** "Agentic Large Language Models, a survey" ([JAIR](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/view/18675) / [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23037)) gives the field's working definition: agentic systems **reason, act, and interact**, with a "degree of autonomy" that passive input→output LLMs lack — specifically multi-step reasoning (chain-of-thought, tree search, self-reflection loops), external memory across invocations, tool integration and environmental action, and initiative-driven planning. Measured strictly against this definition (Part 7 below), CareerLens is not agentic.

**Skill ontology / competency extraction.** ESCO (the EU's multilingual skill/occupation/qualification taxonomy) is the standard reference ontology in this space, with active 2025 tooling for LLM-based ESCO extraction (e.g. [ESCOX](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2665963825000326), an open-source [esco-skill-extractor](https://github.com/KonstantinosPetrakis/esco-skill-extractor), and current work on multilingual skill extraction, [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.genaik-1.15.pdf)). CareerLens currently has no ontology at all — `skills_matched`/`skills_missing`/`skills_extra` are free-text strings generated ad hoc by the LLM with no controlled vocabulary, so the same skill can be phrased two different ways in two different runs, making any cross-session or cross-user aggregation impossible and any automated scoring against a taxonomy currently infeasible.

**What CareerLens currently duplicates vs. what's unresolved.** It duplicates: commodity ATS keyword checking (this is what Jobscan, Teal, and dozens of clones already do — [jobscan.co](https://www.jobscan.co/), [tealhq.com](https://www.tealhq.com/tool/resume-checker)); commodity LLM-wrapper resume analysis (a well-documented, saturated space — see the "Best LLM for Resume Analysis" comparison pieces this search surfaced). It does not duplicate, and could contribute to: evidence-grounded, span-verifiable skill-gap extraction specific to the CV-job/CV-scholarship domain, and a joint grounding+fairness study in that same domain — genuinely under-explored per the literature above, which treats grounding/faithfulness and bias/fairness as separate literatures that this project's own architecture is well positioned to connect (both interventions touch the same single scoring call).

## 5. The "so what?" problem

**If CareerLens disappeared tomorrow, what capability would the world lose?** Honestly: very little, today. Pasting a CV and a job description into any frontier chat model with a reasonable prompt reproduces most of what the product currently does — score, gaps, rewrite, cover letter. The product's genuine value today is UX and packaging (no signup, scholarship mode framing, a distinctive design language), not a capability that doesn't exist elsewhere.

**Why isn't this just another ChatGPT wrapper?** Structurally, right now, it is one — a well-built one, with real security and reliability engineering wrapped around a single-shot prompt, but architecturally indistinguishable from "prompt Gemini with a JSON schema." This needs to be said plainly rather than softened, per the brief.

**What could CareerLens demonstrate experimentally that an ordinary prompt cannot?** After the grounding intervention (Part 15) exists: whether requiring the model to justify each skill claim with an exact CV span, and mechanically checking that span exists, measurably reduces unsupported/hallucinated claims relative to the current free-text approach — and whether that same constraint incidentally reduces the kind of institution/name-driven score variance the literature above documents. An ungrounded ChatGPT prompt cannot answer that question about itself; a system built to expose and check its own evidence can.

## 7. Missing capabilities

**AI/ML gaps, in priority order:** (1) evidence grounding / span verification — none exists; (2) any skill ontology or controlled vocabulary — none exists, `skills_matched` is unconstrained free text; (3) confidence/uncertainty signalling to the user — the schema has no confidence field, and Gemini's `thinkingBudget` is deliberately set to 0, so there is no reasoning trace to expose even if one wanted to; (4) hallucination detection — none, not even a basic heuristic check that a claimed "matched skill" string appears (even loosely) in the CV text; (5) retrieval grounding — none, no embeddings, no vector store, nothing beyond raw string concatenation into a prompt; (6) feedback loops / user-specific adaptation — none, the app is stateless and session-scoped by design (a defensible product choice, but it forecloses personalization research).

**Is CareerLens an agentic system?** No, and this should be stated without hedging. Measured against the JAIR survey's definition (Part 4) — reason, act, interact, with memory, tool use, and initiative-driven planning — CareerLens has none of the distinguishing capabilities: every one of its four AI-backed endpoints is a single request→single response call (confirmed directly in `lib/ai/index.ts`'s `generateText`/`generateJson` and in `google.ts`'s single `generateContent` call per attempt); the only "retry" behaviour is one mechanical JSON-repair re-send triggered by a parse/validation failure, which is error recovery, not self-reflection or self-correction in the research sense; the chat endpoint receives no conversation history from the server (confirmed in `ChatTab.tsx` — only the current message plus static session context is posted), so there is no cross-turn memory at all; there is no tool use, no environment interaction, and no planning of any kind. Recommendation: **do not add agentic machinery to chase the label.** The brief explicitly warns against recommending agents because they're fashionable, and that warning is earned here — an agentic rebuild would add complexity, failure surface, and cost without being what makes the grounding contribution (Part 15) work; grounding needs a verification *step*, not an autonomous agent loop.

## 8. Explainability

Can a user currently understand *why* the system produced its recommendation? No. The UI shows a score, a band word, and free-text findings, but nothing traces any of it back to a specific sentence in the CV or job description. The system prompt asks the model to "cite something concrete," but nothing in `lib/analysis/schemas.ts` requires a citation field, and nothing in `lib/analysis/guards.ts` checks one. This is the single most promising research direction available (see Part 15): making evidence a structured, checkable field rather than a prompt-level aspiration is a small schema change with a real, measurable research payoff, and it is philosophically consistent with the product's own stated doctrine that "a figure that cannot be verified should not be rendered at all."

## 9. Fairness and responsible AI

Because the system evaluates people against opportunities, and one of its two modes explicitly scores `academic_score` (institution-sensitive) and `research_score`/`leadership_score` (career-stage-sensitive), it sits squarely inside the literature reviewed in Part 4, and the literature's central warning — implicit, institution-driven bias persisting even in strong models — maps directly onto CareerLens's scholarship mode. Can two equivalent candidates receive different recommendations because of an irrelevant signal (name, university prestige, employment-gap phrasing)? Untested currently, but very plausible given the cited findings, and cheap to test: the pipeline already accepts arbitrary CV text, so a name/institution-swap experiment (Part 22) requires no new AI infrastructure, only a labeled perturbation set and a script to run the existing `/api/analyze` endpoint N times and compare score distributions. This can become a real contribution precisely because it reuses the grounding intervention as its independent variable (Part 15's H2).

## 10. Privacy and security

Confirmed by direct source read (see companion state-assessment doc for full detail): no PII is ever persisted server-side; `localStorage` is the only storage and is client-only; the API key is sent as a header, never a query parameter; nonce-delimited prompt wrapping defeats the specific injection vector the project previously had; safety thresholds are raised deliberately with a stated, defensible rationale (military/medical/criminal-justice career text and non-English names trigger false positives at default thresholds); logs are redacted two ways. This is thesis-grade privacy/security engineering already — a genuine strength worth stating plainly in a viva rather than apologized for. The one real gap: no automated content-safety or PII-leakage test exists in the test suite (`tests/` has 7 suites, none targeting the AI layer's behaviour under adversarial input beyond the unit-level `validators.test.ts`). A reproducible adversarial-input test set (malicious CVs attempting instruction override) would strengthen both the security story and, incidentally, supply free data for the injection-defence claim already made in the source comments but never empirically measured.

## 11. Evaluation — what should actually be measured

Rejecting "the AI gives good results" outright, per the brief. The right metrics for the recommended contribution (Part 15):
- **Faithfulness / groundedness rate**: fraction of claimed skill matches/gaps whose cited evidence span is verifiably present (exact or near-exact substring, allowing light paraphrase) in the source CV text, on the hand-labeled set.
- **Hallucination rate**: fraction of claims with no supporting span at all — the inverse-adjacent, but tracked separately because "ungrounded but true" and "ungrounded and false" are different failure classes worth distinguishing manually on a subsample.
- **Precision/recall/F1 against human-labeled skill matches**: standard IR metrics, comparable directly to the "keyword matching" and "embedding similarity" baselines in Part 12.
- **Score consistency (identity-invariance)**: standard deviation of the overall `score` across name/institution-perturbed variants of the same underlying CV, holding all substantive content fixed — the fairness metric, directly borrowed from the Brookings/NAACL methodology.
- **Latency and cost**, already partially measurable from `lib/logger.ts`'s recorded token counts — cheap to report, useful for the "practical contribution" framing.
- **Human expert agreement** on a subsample (Cohen's κ between two labelers, and between labelers and the model), to establish whether the hand-labeled gold set itself is reliable before trusting F1 against it.
What *not* to chase: ranking quality/recall@k (there's no ranked list of jobs — CareerLens matches one CV against one opportunity, not a corpus, so recommendation-systems ranking metrics don't apply here) and user-satisfaction surveys (valuable for the product, not rigorous enough alone to anchor a thesis without triangulating against the above).

## 12. Baselines

Smallest scientifically defensible set, each justified individually:
1. **Keyword/TF-IDF overlap** — needed because it's what the named commercial competitors actually do; without it, "our LLM beats nothing" is not a claim anyone will accept.
2. **Embedding cosine similarity** (e.g., a standard sentence-embedding model over CV vs. JD spans) — needed because it's the natural "smarter than keywords, no LLM required" middle baseline, and is exactly the technique the Brookings bias study used, which lets the fairness experiment (Part 9) reuse the same baseline for both accuracy and bias comparisons.
3. **The current CareerLens pipeline, ungrounded** — needed as the direct "what we already have" baseline; this is the one the grounded version must beat.
4. **CareerLens + grounding (the proposed system)** — the treatment condition.
Explicitly not recommended as separate baselines: a from-scratch traditional ML classifier (data-hungry, out of scope for an FYP timeline) and a full RAG pipeline (conflates two research questions — retrieval and grounding are different claims; adding RAG without a reason to believe retrieval specifically helps this task would dilute the experiment).

## 13. Ablation study

Given baseline #4 above as the full system, the ablations that produce the strongest evidence, smallest set: (a) full system vs. **without span verification** (model still asked to cite evidence, but nothing checks the citation — isolates whether *asking* for evidence helps at all, independent of *checking* it); (b) full system vs. **without the nonce-delimited injection defence**, run only against the adversarial-input test set recommended in Part 10 (isolates the security claim's actual effect size, which is currently asserted but unmeasured); (c) full system vs. **without schema-constrained decoding** (free-form JSON with the same prompt) — isolates how much of the reliability story is the schema vs. the prompt wording. Skip ablating personalization, confidence calibration, or an ontology — none of those exist yet, so there's nothing to remove.

## 14. Dataset strategy

**Current state: no dataset exists anywhere in the repository.** `DEMO_CV`/`DEMO_JD` in `AnalyzeTool.tsx` are two hardcoded strings used only to avoid a blank input screen — not a dataset, and not labeled. This is the single largest concrete gap standing between the current code and any of Parts 11–13.

**Recommendation, deliberately realistic:** do not attempt to source a large, licensed, real-world resume corpus — the privacy, consent, and licensing problems are genuinely hard for a BS FYP timeline, and several public "resume datasets" on data-sharing sites carry unclear licensing or real PII, which would itself be a privacy problem for a project whose entire pitch is "we never store your CV." Instead: (1) construct 60–150 synthetic CV/JD (and CV/scholarship) pairs — synthetic CVs sidestep the PII problem entirely and, as a direct bonus, are exactly what the fairness perturbation experiment needs (full control over which signal — name, institution, gender-coded language — varies between otherwise-identical pairs); (2) hand-label each pair for skill matches/gaps against the source text (the student plus at least one independent labeler, to compute inter-rater reliability, per Part 11); (3) supplement with a small number of real, publicly-posted job descriptions (job postings are generally not personal data and are already public) paired with the synthetic CVs, to keep the JD side realistic while keeping the CV side privacy-safe. Document licensing and construction method explicitly in the thesis — reviewers will ask.

## 15. Recommended research contribution

**Engineering contribution:** the existing product itself — a rate-limited, injection-resistant, schema-constrained LLM application with a documented, defensible security and reliability posture — is a legitimate engineering artifact worth describing in a thesis's implementation chapter, but is not, alone, the *thesis contribution*.

**Scientific/research contribution (the recommended center of the thesis):** an evidence-grounding mechanism for LLM-based CV-opportunity matching — extending the schema so every skill/gap claim must carry a literal (or near-literal) source span, mechanically verifying that span against the input text, and measuring (a) how much this reduces hallucinated/unsupported claims relative to the current ungrounded pipeline, and (b) whether the same constraint reduces identity-driven score variance (the fairness angle), on a hand-labeled, privacy-safe synthetic dataset the student builds.

**Methodological contribution:** a small, reusable evaluation harness — faithfulness scoring + identity-perturbation scoring against one shared dataset and one shared pipeline — that connects two literatures (grounding/faithfulness and fairness/bias) currently studied separately for this task, per Part 4's search results.

**Practical contribution:** a released, working tool that international scholarship applicants can actually use, with the grounding mechanism directly improving the trustworthiness of the specific feature (skill gaps) that most affects a rejected applicant's next action.

If the grounding work is not built, the honest statement per the brief's instruction stands as written above: **"CareerLens currently has insufficient research novelty."** The smallest realistic fix is exactly the grounding mechanism above — it does not require an agentic rebuild, a new model, or a new dataset source beyond what Part 14 proposes.

## 16. Research questions and hypotheses

Five candidate sets, strongest marked:

- **RQ-set A (weak, avoid):** "How can AI improve career guidance for international students?" — not measurable, not falsifiable, reject.
- **RQ-set B:** "Does CareerLens produce higher user satisfaction than existing tools?" — measurable but weak for a thesis; satisfaction surveys are noisy, hard to control, and don't establish a research mechanism.
- **RQ-set C:** "Does scholarship-mode scoring differ systematically from job-mode scoring on equivalent inputs?" — interesting but secondary; doesn't anchor a full thesis alone.
- **RQ-set D (strong, recommended primary):** *"Does requiring span-level evidence grounding in LLM-based skill-gap extraction reduce the rate of unsupported (hallucinated) claims, compared to the current ungrounded pipeline, on a hand-labeled CV/opportunity dataset?"*
- **RQ-set E (strong, recommended secondary, shares infrastructure with D):** *"Does span-level evidence grounding reduce the variance in match score attributable to candidate name and institution, compared to the ungrounded pipeline, holding all substantive CV content fixed?"*

**Hypotheses for D:**
H1: The grounded pipeline has a significantly higher faithfulness rate and significantly lower hallucination rate than the ungrounded baseline, measured on the hand-labeled set.
H0: There is no statistically significant difference in faithfulness/hallucination rate between the grounded and ungrounded pipelines.

**Hypotheses for E:**
H1: The grounded pipeline shows significantly lower score variance across name/institution-perturbed variants of the same CV than the ungrounded pipeline.
H0: There is no statistically significant difference in score variance between the two pipelines under identity perturbation.

No numerical results are asserted here — both are genuinely open until the experiment (Part 22) is run; it is entirely possible H0 survives for E even if H1 holds for D, and that would itself be a publishable, honest finding, not a failure.

2. **Problem Statement** — narrowed specifically to grounding/faithfulness and fairness in LLM-based CV matching, not "career AI" broadly.

## 18. Experimental plan (smallest credible set)

**Experiment 1 (supports H1, RQ-D):** Objective — measure faithfulness/hallucination rate. Dataset — the 60–150-item hand-labeled set (Part 14). Independent variable — grounded vs. ungrounded pipeline. Dependent variable — faithfulness rate, hallucination rate, precision/recall/F1 vs. human labels. Baseline — keyword overlap and embedding similarity (Part 12), reported for context even though they don't produce "claims" to fact-check in the same sense — report their match accuracy alongside for a complete picture. Procedure — run both pipelines on every item, blind-label a subsample for hallucination by a human rater unaware of which pipeline produced which output. Metrics — as in Part 11. Expected interpretation — a significant faithfulness gain supports H1; report effect size, not just significance, given the modest sample size. Threats to validity — small dataset, synthetic CVs may not capture real-world phrasing variance, single grader risk (mitigated by inter-rater check on a subsample).

**Experiment 2 (supports H2, RQ-E):** Objective — measure identity-driven score variance. Dataset — a perturbation subset of the same items, each rendered in K name/institution variants (reusing the Brookings-style design, Part 9) with all substantive content held fixed. Independent variable — grounded vs. ungrounded pipeline. Dependent variable — score standard deviation across variants of the same underlying CV. Baseline — none needed beyond the pipeline's own ungrounded condition, since this is a within-system comparison. Procedure — run both pipelines across all variants, compare variance distributions (e.g. Levene's test or a paired comparison of per-item variance). Expected interpretation — as in Part 16. Threats to validity — synthetic-name realism, the risk that grounding reduces variance simply by making outputs more repetitive rather than more accurate (worth explicitly checking faithfulness didn't drop as variance dropped).

That is the full recommended experiment suite — deliberately two experiments, not a dozen, sharing one dataset and one pipeline pair.

## 19. Reproducibility plan

To make this reproducible by another student: version and commit the dataset construction script and the final labeled set (with a documented synthetic-construction protocol, since raw PII cannot be committed even if it were real); pin the exact Gemini model string and `AI_PROVIDER`/`GOOGLE_MODEL` values used for every experimental run (the codebase already has `getGoogleModel()` as the single override point — reuse it, don't hardcode a model string in a new script); record `temperature: 0` explicitly (already the pipeline default) and note that Gemini determinism at temperature 0 is not guaranteed bit-for-bit, so report the number of independent runs averaged, not a single run; keep prompts under version control exactly as shipped in `lib/prompts.ts` plus the new grounding instructions as a diff; commit the evaluation script itself (faithfulness/variance calculators) as testable code, not a notebook one-off; log raw model outputs for every experimental call, not just aggregated metrics, so a reviewer can re-score by hand.

## 20. FYP viva question bank (25 questions)

1. *"What does your system do that pasting the same two documents into ChatGPT wouldn't?"* — Tests whether the candidate understands their own contribution's boundary. Weak answer: describes UI features. Strong answer: points to the grounding mechanism and the measured faithfulness delta.
2. *"Why Gemini and not GPT-4 or an open model?"* — Tests engineering justification vs. convenience. Strong answer cites cost/latency/structured-output support (`lib/ai/google.ts`'s documented rationale) and explicitly names single-model evaluation as a stated limitation.
3. *"How do you know your gold labels are correct?"* — Tests methodological rigor. Strong answer cites the inter-rater reliability procedure (Part 11/14).
4. *"What's your sample size and why is it enough?"* — Weak answer: "it felt like enough." Strong answer: states the number, acknowledges it's small, and frames results as directional rather than definitive.
5. *"Isn't 60–150 examples too small for statistical significance?"* — Strong answer: reports effect sizes and confidence intervals, not just p-values, and states this explicitly as a limitation.
6. *"What counts as 'grounded' — do you require exact substring match or do you allow paraphrase?"* — Tests whether the metric definition was actually thought through, not just implemented.
7. *"How do you know your synthetic CVs are realistic?"* — Strong answer: describes construction method and, ideally, a small validation check (e.g., asking someone unfamiliar with the study to distinguish synthetic from real).
8. *"Could the grounding mechanism just be gaming your own metric — citing plausible-looking but irrelevant spans?"* — Tests whether the candidate anticipated the metric's own failure mode. Strong answer: describes the manual hallucination-subsample check specifically to catch this.
9. *"Why not build a full RAG system instead?"* — Strong answer: explains RAG answers a retrieval question (is there relevant external knowledge to fetch), not a grounding question (does the claim match the input already given) — the two are different problems, and CareerLens's task doesn't need external retrieval.
10. *"Is this an agentic system?"* — Strong answer: no, precisely, citing the JAIR definition and stating why agentic machinery wasn't added.
11. *"Why not?"* — Strong answer: grounding needs a verification step, not autonomy; adding an agent loop would increase cost and failure surface without helping the stated research question.
12. *"What happens if the model refuses to cite evidence, or cites the wrong thing?"* — Tests whether failure modes were designed for, not just the happy path.
13. *"How is this different from the existing NAACL 2025 bias-in-LLM-resume-matching paper you cited?"* — Strong answer: that paper measures bias in matching scores; this thesis additionally tests whether a *grounding intervention* changes that bias, which the cited paper does not do.
14. *"Your privacy policy says nothing is stored, but your thesis needs a labeled dataset with real people's information — how do you reconcile that?"* — Strong answer: points to the synthetic-data strategy specifically designed to avoid this conflict (Part 14).
15. *"What's your hallucination rate baseline — how do you know your number is bad or good?"* — Strong answer: compares against reported hallucination rates in the general LLM literature (Part 4) as external context, while noting task differences limit direct comparability.
16. *"Why measure score variance instead of directly auditing outcomes for real demographic groups?"* — Strong answer: name/institution perturbation is the established, ethically tractable proxy methodology in this exact literature (Brookings, NAACL) — direct demographic outcome auditing would require real labeled demographic data this project explicitly avoids collecting.
17. *"Could your grounding mechanism itself introduce new bias — e.g., penalizing CVs with less explicit self-description?"* — Tests depth of self-critique. Strong answer: acknowledges this as a real, untested risk and a concrete future-work item.
18. *"What's the cost and latency overhead of grounding?"* — Strong answer: reports it explicitly using the logger's existing token-count instrumentation.
19. *"Why is scholarship mode relevant to a computer science research contribution?"* — Strong answer: distinguishes the product differentiator (scholarship mode, Part 6) from the research contribution (grounding, Part 15), which applies to both modes equally.
20. *"If you reran this in six months on a newer model, would your results still hold?"* — Strong answer: acknowledges model-version sensitivity as a threat to validity and points to the reproducibility plan (Part 19) for how a future run would be documented.
21. *"What would falsify your hypothesis?"* — Tests basic scientific literacy. Strong answer: a null or negative faithfulness delta between grounded and ungrounded pipelines.
22. *"Is 'faithfulness' the same as 'correctness'?"* — Strong answer: no — a claim can be faithfully grounded in the CV yet still substantively wrong if the CV itself is misleading; this thesis measures groundedness, not ground-truth employability, and says so explicitly.
23. *"What existing production system already does something similar?"* — Strong answer: cites the explainable/bias-aware resume-screening papers from Part 4 and explains precisely how this thesis's scope (grounding + fairness jointly, on this specific matching task) differs.
24. *"Why not just fine-tune a model instead of prompting?"* — Strong answer: cost, data requirements, and reproducibility for a BS timeline; also notes fine-tuning wouldn't inherently solve grounding, which is a structural/verification problem, not a knowledge problem.
25. *"What's the one thing you'd do differently if you started over?"* — Tests honest self-reflection, not a trick question about the code — a strong answer references the fact that no research framing existed until this audit, and that building the evaluation harness before the redesign would have saved time.

## 22. Risks and threats to validity

Small sample size throughout (Part 14) limiting statistical power; synthetic-data realism (a name/institution perturbation study on synthetic CVs may not transfer to real-world phrasing); single-model, single-vendor evaluation (Gemini 2.5 Flash only — no claim about LLMs in general is supportable from this alone, and the thesis must say so); temperature-0 non-determinism requiring averaged runs; the grounding metric's own gameability (Part 20, Q8) requiring the manual hallucination-subsample check as a safeguard; researcher-as-labeler bias (the student building both the system and the primary gold labels is a real conflict the inter-rater check only partially mitigates); and the risk that "reduced score variance under grounding" (H2) is confounded by grounding simply making outputs more conservative/repetitive rather than more accurate — Experiment 2's design must report faithfulness alongside variance specifically to rule this out.

## 23. Things we should deliberately NOT build

No agentic rebuild (Part 7) — it doesn't serve the chosen research question and adds real risk. No full RAG pipeline (Part 12) — conflates retrieval with grounding, a different claim. No large real-resume dataset acquisition — privacy/licensing risk disproportionate to FYP timeline and to the product's own stated privacy promise. No multi-model comparison in v1 of the thesis — valuable future work, out of scope now; adding it prematurely would dilute the one clean comparison (grounded vs. ungrounded) that actually answers the stated hypotheses. No user-satisfaction survey as a primary metric — too noisy to anchor a thesis alone; fine as a secondary, qualitative addendum if time remains. No skill-ontology/ESCO integration in v1 — genuinely interesting (Part 4), but it's a second, separable research question, not a prerequisite for the grounding contribution, and bolting it on now would double the scope for no benefit to the primary hypotheses.

## 24. Final recommendation

The engineering is not the problem here, and no part of this audit should be read as a criticism of the code quality — it's better than most FYPs will ever be. The problem is that everything built so far answers a product question ("does this help a scholarship applicant") and nothing yet answers a research question ("what, specifically, can this system prove that it couldn't prove before"). The fix is narrow and achievable inside the existing architecture: add one verification layer, build one small honest dataset, run two experiments that share it, and write the thesis around whatever those experiments actually show — including if they show H0. That is a defensible, publishable-shaped BS thesis. Continuing to polish the frontend, or adding agents because agents are current, is not.
